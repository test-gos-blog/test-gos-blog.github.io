Google [defines](https://developer.android.com/google/play/integrity/overview) Play Integrity on their website as follows:

> The Play Integrity API helps you check that user actions and server requests are coming from your genuine app, installed by Google Play, running on a genuine and certified Android device. By detecting risky interactions — like those from tampered app versions, untrustworthy devices, or emulated environments — your backend server can respond with appropriate actions to prevent abuse and unauthorized access, fight fraud, combat cheating, and protect users from attacks.

On the surface, Play Integrity may seem like a very powerful tool for developers who want to ensure their apps are running on a device and operating system they know is safe to reduce abuse. For example, banks may want to protect their customers from accidentally leaking their credentials on an unsafe device. Social media companies may want to avoid people using modified versions of their apps to evade bans or violate other rules. Play Integrity doesn't directly prevent attacks or abuse. An app can invoke the Play Integrity API to receive a [Play Integrity Verdict](https://developer.android.com/google/play/integrity/verdicts), and developers may design their apps to behave differently based on those verdicts.

Play Integrity is entirely optional and app developers aren't required to use it to list their apps on the Play Store. However, Google has been increasingly advising app developers to use it, supposedly to reduce abuse. As the feature is becoming more widely adopted, an increasing number of GrapheneOS users are encountering issues using certain apps. Reported issues can range from certain features not working to apps refusing to run at all.

## Security Feature or Theater?

In some places, Google does use the word "security" when referring to Play Integrity, for example it appears in this [security checklist](https://developer.android.com/privacy-and-security/security-tips#app_integrity) in Android documentation. It is understandable that app developers would be happy to let Google, a company well known for its dedication to security, help them further secure their apps against abuse, but calling Play Integrity a security feature is a stretch at best.

Unfortunately, when it comes to security, Play Integrity has very lax criteria. One example is that Google allows ancient devices with years of missing security patches to receive "strong" integrity verdicts. Without regular security patches for the OS, firmware, drivers, etc., a device or OS is very insecure. According to [Android documentation](https://developer.android.com/google/play/integrity/verdicts#device-integrity-field) the highest tier, the "strong" integrity level, permits being up to a year behind on the official dates for security patches for Android 13 and higher and indefinitely far behind for older versions, even being eight years out of date.

Unlike many operating systems based on AOSP, GrapheneOS quickly applies patches and quickly ports to newer Android versions. While major OEMs and other OSes based on AOSP may take months to fully apply security patches or port to newer releases, GrapheneOS often ships updates within days. Additionally, GrapheneOS has [exploit protections](https://grapheneos.org/features#exploit-protection) that other operating systems do not have or take advantage of. GrapheneOS is objectively more secure than operating systems that can receive "strong" Play Integrity verdicts.

## GMS Licensing and Lock-in

"Strong" integrity verdicts are returned only for operating systems signed by companies that license Google Mobile Services ("GMS") and follow Google's rules for Android licensing. This means that other operating systems that don't license GMS can get only the "basic" integrity verdict, or no verdicts at all which can lead to apps not working as expected. Users who experience friction when trying to use apps on their devices can get frustrated and might switch to another OS, perhaps one that has weaker privacy and security. Play Integrity is proving to be very good at indirectly dissuading people from using operating systems that don't license GMS.

Since GrapheneOS does not license GMS, it can pass only the "basic" integrity verdict. According to Google's [documentation](https://developer.android.com/google/play/integrity/verdicts#optional-device-labels), that means "The app is running on a device that passes 'basic' system integrity checks. [...] On Android 13 and higher, the `MEETS_BASIC_INTEGRITY` verdict requires only that the attestation [root of trust](https://developer.android.com/privacy-and-security/security-key-attestation#root_certificate) is provided by Google." Despite this description, we've found that in order to get the "basic" integrity verdict, GrapheneOS users with Sandboxed Google Play installed must both be logged in to Google Play and have installed the app from the Google Play Store. If the user's setup doesn't meet both criteria, they may not be able to run apps expecting at least the "basic" integrity verdict. One good example of how this negatively affects GrapheneOS users is the recent wave of people banned from using WhatsApp. People who installed WhatsApp via the APK on WhatsApp's website or via Aurora were banned while those who installed WhatsApp using Google Play Store were unaffected.

If the goal is to protect users, it doesn't make sense to categorize GrapheneOS as if it were easier to abuse or more susceptible to fraud than other operating systems based on AOSP. GrapheneOS "checks all the boxes" for user safety and arguably should be eligible to receive "strong" integrity verdicts. Google is in a position where they alone get to decide which integrity verdicts are returned, and there's no standard that OSes can meet to start receiving strong integrity verdicts. By convincing more app developers to implement checks using Play Integrity along with other features and wide adoption of their other services, Google is effectively consolidating more control over both OEMs and users.

## A Better Alternative

Google's Play Integrity locks out competition and dissuades people from using alternative operating systems that don't license GMS, but there's a better alternative to it: Android's hardware attestation. Although hardware attestation is biased towards stock operating systems, it allows alternatives and supports arbitrary roots of trust. Using Android's hardware attestation API instead of Play Integrity gives app developers a way to verify operating systems without blocking them just because they don't license GMS.

Using hardware attestation gives developers more control than relying solely on Play Integrity. We provide a guide on [our website](https://grapheneos.org/articles/attestation-compatibility-guide) for developers to move away from or complement Play Integrity, and we recently [announced](https://grapheneos.social/@GrapheneOS/116439642834521772) a JSON API to make it even easier for apps or services to permit GrapheneOS. If required, developers can easily use hardware attestation to selectively permit operating systems meeting their requirements, not Google's.

GrapheneOS exists to improve privacy and security on mobile devices and we appreciate when others, including Google, make or provide privacy or security features. But Play Integrity is not a privacy or safety tool, because it accepts many unsafe software configurations as long as they are supported by a business relationship while rejecting third-party software that is more secure. The result is increased friction for users of AOSP-based operating systems that don't license Google Mobile Services and actively discourages competition.
